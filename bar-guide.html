Home bar shopping guide

Buy fewer bottles and make better drinks

A good home bar is not a wall of bottles. It is a balanced shelf with a few reliable spirits, fresh citrus, useful sweeteners, bitters, cold mixers, and tools that make precise drinks easy.

Core shelf

Start with bottles that unlock families of cocktails

Gin

Buy a London dry gin first because it works in Martinis, Negronis, Collins drinks, sours, and herb-driven cocktails.

Whiskey

Choose bourbon for rounder drinks and rye for drier stirred cocktails. Either one gives you Old Fashioneds, Whiskey Sours, Manhattans, and Boulevardiers.

Tequila

Start with blanco tequila for Margaritas, Palomas, and citrus-forward drinks. Add reposado later when you want richer stirred agave cocktails.

Rum

White rum covers Daiquiris and Mojitos. Aged or dark rum adds depth for Mai Tais, Jungle Birds, Painkillers, and ginger beer highballs.

Modifiers

Buy supporting bottles by usefulness, not novelty

Sweet vermouth

Sweet vermouth is essential for Manhattans, Negronis, Boulevardiers, and Americanos. Keep it refrigerated after opening and replace it when it tastes flat or oxidized.

Dry vermouth

Dry vermouth is the backbone of a Martini. Buy a bottle you would drink chilled on its own because it has nowhere to hide in simple cocktails.

Orange liqueur

Orange liqueur is one of the highest-value cocktail bottles because it supports Margaritas, Sidecars, Cosmos, Mai Tais, and many classic sours.

Bitter aperitifs and amari

Campari and Aperol bring bitterness, color, and structure. Start with Campari if you like Negronis and Boulevardiers, then add Aperol for lighter spritzes and modern sours.

Fresh ingredients

Fresh citrus and simple syrup matter more than expensive bottles

Limes

Keep limes on hand for Margaritas, Daiquiris, Mojitos, Palomas, and tiki drinks. Juice them close to serving because fresh lime changes quickly.

Lemons

Lemons carry Whiskey Sours, French 75s, Bee's Knees, Collins drinks, and many zero-proof builds. They also give you garnish twists for stirred cocktails.

Simple syrup

Make simple syrup with equal parts sugar and hot water. Store it cold, label the date, and replace it when it loses freshness.

Herbs and produce

Mint, cucumber, ginger, oranges, and grapefruit turn a basic shelf into a flexible one. Buy them when you plan to use them within a few days.

Bitters, mixers, and tools

Small purchases can make the whole shelf work harder

Bitters

Angostura bitters should be the first bottle. Add orange bitters for Martinis and stirred whiskey drinks, then add Peychaud's bitters if you make Sazeracs.

Mixers

Buy soda water, ginger beer, grapefruit soda, tonic, and sparkling wine in small formats when possible. Smaller containers stay fizzy and reduce waste.

Ice

Clear, dense ice improves texture and dilution. Use large cubes for spirit-forward drinks and smaller ice for shaken drinks, highballs, and tiki builds.

Tools

A jigger, shaker, mixing glass, bar spoon, Hawthorne strainer, fine strainer, peeler, and citrus press are enough for a serious home bar.

Glassware

The right glass changes how a cocktail looks and tastes

Glass shape controls temperature, carbonation, ice, and how much aroma reaches your nose, so it shapes the drink as much as the recipe does. You don't need a full set. Start with a coupe, a rocks glass, and a highball, which together serve 32 of our 43 recipes.

Chill what's served up

Fill the glass with ice and water while you mix, or keep coupes in the freezer. A cold glass holds the drink at the temperature you shook it to.

Match the pour to the glass

A drink that fills about three-quarters of the glass looks right and carries without spilling. A small pour in a huge glass warms up fast.

Rinse well

Soap film flattens bubbles and collapses the foam on egg-white drinks. Rinse thoroughly and let glasses air dry.

Coupe

4.5 to 6 oz · Shallow, rounded bowl on a stem

Best for: Shaken sours and stirred drinks served up, with no ice in the glass.

The stem keeps your hand from warming the drink, and the wide bowl lets aroma open up as you sip. Unlike a V-shaped martini glass it doesn't spill when you carry it.

Tip: A Nick & Nora is a fine swap. Chill it in the freezer for 15 minutes before pouring.

17 drinks here: Aviation, Bee's Knees, Bijou, Clover Club, Corpse Reviver No. 2, Cosmopolitan, Daiquiri, Division Bell, Eastside, Espresso Martini, Last Word, Manhattan, Naked and Famous, Paper Plane, Remember the Maine, Siesta, Southside

Martini glass

5 to 7 oz · Wide V-shaped cone on a stem

Best for: Spirit-forward drinks served up, like the Martini and Vesper.

The wide surface lifts the aroma of a lemon twist or olive and keeps a very cold drink looking sharp. It warms fast, so pour smaller drinks and finish them quickly.

Tip: Fill to a few millimeters below the rim. The cone shape makes a full pour easy to spill.

2 drinks here: Dry Martini, Vesper

Flute

6 oz · Tall, narrow bowl on a stem

Best for: Sparkling cocktails like the French 75.

A narrow opening exposes less surface, so bubbles last longer and the aroma funnels toward your nose. Tilt the glass when you top with sparkling wine to keep the fizz.

Tip: Pour the base first and top with the bubbles last, slowly down the side.

1 drink here: French 75

Rocks glass

8 to 12 oz · Short, wide, heavy-bottomed tumbler

Best for: Stirred whiskey drinks, Negronis, and sours poured over ice.

The wide mouth leaves room for one large cube. Big ice melts slowly, so the drink stays cold without watering down, and you can express a citrus peel right over the surface.

Tip: Make clear large cubes in a silicone mold. One big cube beats a handful of small ones.

9 drinks here: Boulevardier, Mai Tai, Margarita, Negroni, Oaxaca Old Fashioned, Old Fashioned, Penicillin, Sazerac, Whiskey Sour

Highball

10 to 12 oz · Tall, straight-sided tumbler

Best for: Drinks topped with soda, ginger beer, or grapefruit soda.

Tall and narrow keeps carbonation from escaping and packs the ice tight, so the drink stays cold and fizzy to the last sip.

Tip: Fill with ice to the top first. More ice melts slower than less ice.

6 drinks here: Americano, Dark 'n' Stormy, Garibaldi, Mojito, No-Groni Spritz, Paloma

Collins glass

12 to 14 oz · Taller and slimmer than a highball

Best for: Long fizzes and Collins-style drinks with lots of soda.

The extra height gives a frothy fizz room to rise into a proper head, and carries more soda without going flat.

Tip: For egg-white or cream fizzes, top with soda slowly so the foam lifts above the rim.

2 drinks here: Ginger Tea Collins, Ramos Gin Fizz

Wine glass

12 to 20 oz · Large stemmed bowl

Best for: Spritzes and other lightly sparkling, ice-filled drinks.

The big bowl holds plenty of ice and lets the citrus and herbal aromas gather above the drink, while the stem keeps your hand off the cold bowl.

Tip: A stemless wine glass works nearly as well, and is harder to knock over.

2 drinks here: Aperol Spritz, Garden Spritz

Copper mug

12 to 16 oz · Handled mug, traditionally copper

Best for: Moscow Mules and other ginger beer drinks.

Copper conducts cold quickly, so the mug frosts over and keeps the drink icy. The handle also keeps your hand off the cold metal.

Tip: Choose a mug lined with nickel or stainless steel. Acidic drinks like lime juice shouldn't sit in bare copper.

1 drink here: Moscow Mule

Tiki mug or hurricane glass

12 to 20 oz · Large, bulbous mug or curvy tall glass

Best for: Tropical drinks poured over crushed or pebble ice.

Thick walls insulate against melting crushed ice, and the big volume leaves room for juice-heavy drinks and a generous garnish canopy of mint, pineapple, and nutmeg.

Tip: Any tall glass can stand in. Fill with crushed ice and let the garnish do the showing off.

3 drinks here: Jungle Bird, Painkiller, Piña Colada

Buying order

Build in rounds instead of buying everything at once

  1. Buy one base spirit, fresh citrus, simple syrup, and Angostura bitters.
  2. Add one modifier that pairs with your base spirit, such as sweet vermouth for whiskey or orange liqueur for tequila.
  3. Add one sparkling mixer that creates longer drinks for guests.
  4. Add a second base spirit only after you know which drinks you make most often.
  5. Add specialty bottles when they unlock recipes you already want to make.
